WHAT SETS THE SIZE, which is the question everyone has: three things multiplied.

The learning rate, 1/16, is a number we chose. On the CoCo it is four shift instructions, which is why it is a sixteenth and not something tidier.

How wrong it was, 0.9655, is one minus what it gave the right answer. That term is the self-correcting one: as the model improves it shrinks, so the nudges shrink themselves. Nobody turns that down. It is why the thing settles instead of oscillating.

The incoming number decides how much each weight is to blame. A weight fed by a big number contributed more to the wrong score, so it moves more.

FIRST CODE IN THE TALK. This is what was running behind block 2's slides, and it has just finished.

The 6809 has a MUL instruction and it multiplies two UNSIGNED bytes. The model needs signed multiplication. The first version did it a bit at a time: correct, easy to explain, and 38.6 million instructions for the run.

This is what replaced it. Split the 16-bit operand, two MULs, add the halves. 15.8 million instructions. The model is bit-for-bit identical: not one output changed.

THE REGISTER COLUMN is a captured training step, not an invention: SINCLAIR's weight update at epoch 5, context 26 times error -244. The MUL rows split D into A : B, so both of addb's operands are on screen before it runs: the 230 is the second MUL's B, and the stored 1 is the first product's high byte, two rows up. Two things to point at. B holds 12 and then 255 — those are -244's two bytes taken unsigned, because MUL never sees a sign. And the second MUL's A half, 25, is never stored: the low sixteen bits are the whole answer. The product this walk ends on, -6344, is the exact gradient the shift slides are about to divide by sixteen. One update, traced end to end.

THE POINT, and it is a human one: the learning algorithm did not change. Somebody understood both the mathematics and the machine, and that is what turned a correct experiment into something you can watch happen.

MUL is unsigned, so if the factor was negative its unsigned form is 256 too large. Subtract the multiplier's low byte from the high byte of the result and it is correct again.

And make the connection back two slides. These same sixteen bits read as 59,192 if you take them as unsigned. Nothing in the bits says which. THE INSTRUCTION YOU CHOOSE DECIDES. POINT AT THE LEFT EDGE and step the rows. The sign bit stays 1 and copies itself downward, one more each time. That is what "arithmetic" shift means, and it is why -6344 halves to -3172 instead of turning into some large positive number. LSRA, the logical shift, would put a 0 there and -6344 would become 29,596.

THEN POINT AT THE MIDDLE. Watch bits cross the gap: what falls out of A arrives at the top of B. That is the whole reason there are two different instructions instead of two ASRs.

-6344, -3172, -1586, -793, -397. The weight goes from 683 to 1080.

The bits falling off the right in red are thrown away: 0, 0, 0, 1. That is the rounding error of a learning rate. It accumulates over 1,160 updates, and it is also why the whole thing fits and runs.

IF ASKED WHY NOT ASRA AND ASRB, which an assembly-literate room will: ASRB sign-extends B instead of taking the carry, so the bit that fell out of A is thrown away and B's own top bit is duplicated. You get two independent 8-bit shifts. Verified on the direct simulator: halving $0175 gives 186 with asra/rorb and 58 with asra/asrb.

Nasty detail worth having: on some values the two agree, whenever A's dropped bit happens to equal B's top bit. That is a bug that survives casual testing.
