THE QUESTION EVERYONE ASKS. Answer it before they have to. First half is conceptual: one number can only say how much of one thing a word is. Three numbers give it a position in a space, so words can be close in more than one way at once. Modern models use thousands of numbers per word. Same idea, more room. Second half is the machine. Every extra number is another 29 by 3 multiplies per example, and a MUL costs 11 cycles at 0.89 MHz. THEN THE HONEST BIT, and do not skip it: six would have fit. The multiply floor at three is under four seconds. The machine was not the constraint here. Three is the smallest we tried and it worked, and the rule was expand only when the evidence says quality is insufficient. It never said that. So the width is a decision somebody made, not a limit the hardware imposed. Hold that thought for the last slide. The model that DID blow the budget was the character-level one: 12.8 million multiplies, 158 seconds of bare MUL against 180. That is what moving from letters to words bought, and it is a 42-fold reduction. SAY "FLOOR" OUT LOUD. These are multiply instructions only. Nothing here is a measured runtime.

Two window positions, 29 tokens, three numbers each. 174 of the model's 290 parameters are these two tables.

ANSWERS "where did that row come from". It came from here. Two tables BECAUSE THE CONTEXT WINDOW IS TWO TOKENS WIDE. One table per position in the window. Say that before anything else on this slide; it is the only reason the screen is split in half. 29 words in each, three numbers per word. That is 174 numbers, and the model only has 290, so most of this model is a lookup table. Point at COMMODORE in both columns. Same word, two different rows. THAT is what "positional" means, and it is why COMMODORE AMIGA and AMIGA COMMODORE are not the same thing to the machine. If anyone asks how it knows about word order, this slide is the answer. The context is then COMMODORE, so it fetches the top row of the left table and the COMMODORE row of the right one, and adds them. No arithmetic beyond the addition. These numbers are random right now. Training is what makes them mean something.

What a parameter is

A parameter is one number that training is allowed to change. This model has 290. GPT-3 had 175,000,000,000. Same word, same meaning.

THE OTHER WORD EVERYONE HAS HEARD. Nobody has had it explained. We just chose 3 numbers per token. This is what that choice multiplies up to. Step the three lines and let the arithmetic do the work. The 2 in the first line is the context window. Every term in these sums is a decision somebody made: how wide the window is, how many tokens exist, how many numbers describe each one. 174 is the table they just saw. 87 is a second table, one row per word it can predict. 29 is a starting nudge per word. 290 in total, and every one accounted for. THE DEFINITION, say it plainly: a parameter is one number that training is allowed to change. That is all it has ever meant. Then the scale line. 290 against GPT-3's 175 billion. Do not editorialise; let them sit with it. Same word, same meaning, six hundred million times as many. Note that vocabulary and numbers-per-token both appear in every line. Step through the epochs. Nonsense, then a first-word habit, then shape, then TANDY MODEL 100 four times out of four. DO NOT SKIP THE LAST ONE. By epoch 20 it has collapsed onto one answer and more training does not help. The loss keeps falling and the output stops improving. Same lesson as the epoch sweep in EXP-007: more training is not more useful, and we only know that because we measured the behaviour we cared about instead of the number the optimizer gave us. THEN THE BUDGET, and this is the point of the slide. Two numbers were chosen along the way, 20 epochs and 290 parameters, and each one is a bill that had to be paid. Epochs buy time. 20 by 58 by 261 is 302,760 multiplies, and 15.8 million instructions for the whole run. Parameters occupy memory, and the model does not get the machine to itself. 580 bytes of weights, 3,355 of code, 234 of working space: 4,169 bytes that have to coexist with BASIC, the screen, and anything else running. On a 32K machine that is the constraint that bites when you make the vocabulary or the context window bigger. DO NOT QUOTE A RUNTIME. The target was three minutes and we have a cycle projection, not a measurement. Say that out loud; it is on the slide. Land it: neither number is magic. Somebody picked each one so the whole thing would run in a reasonable time and fit in a reasonable amount of memory. That is what designing a model IS, and it is the same decision being made today at a different scale. The 60-epoch row is evidence that 20 was enough. It is not a rival choice. If asked how long it takes: it has not been timed on hardware. That is the honest answer and it is the next thing to fix.

Now you play it

EXP-013 · the game opponent that learns

NEVER CUT. Someone from the room calls throws. It does not know the rules: RULES 0/25 says so. It does not know the player either: MEMORY n/rounds says how little it has. Both stay on screen. Six or eight rounds. It states its expectation BEFORE each throw. Around twenty-five rounds it has the rules; it never finishes learning the person. Press R in front of them and let it fall over. QUOTE THE HUMAN NUMBER: 80% against six synthetic players with habits. 52.8% against a recorded 200-round session with a person who was trying. Better than chance at three sigma, nowhere near enough to win. Five of six synthetic players had a habit and one did not. A person plays like the one that did not. The test set encoded an assumption about people and the average hid it. 100 bytes. No neural network. A tiny table beat every model we tried, which is why there is no model in there at all.
